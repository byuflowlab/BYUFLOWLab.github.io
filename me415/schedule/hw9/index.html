HW 9

We revisit the Saturn V, but this ftime ocused on the flight trajectory. We can’t use the analytic rocket equation because the flight angle, thrust, drag, and specific impulse change in time. Instead, we need to use numerical integration. The ODEs you need to integrate (for \(V, m, z, x\)), including drag, are given at the end of the Tsiolkovsky Rocket Equation section of the textbook.

As mentioned, the heading angle (the angle of the velocity vector measured from vertical) changes significantly throughout the flight. I fit a curve to postflight trajectory data from Apollo 10 and computed the heading angle as a function of time during stage 1. In this fit \(\theta\) is in radians, \(t\) is the time since liftoff in seconds, and \(\theta = 0\) corresponds to vertical flight:

\(\theta = p_1 \arctan\left(p_2 t^{p_3}\right)\) where \(p_1 = 0.866, p_2 = 2.665 \times 10^{-5}, p_3 = 2.378\)

The thrust also changes during the flight. Each of the five engines burns propellant at a nearly constant rate of 2647 kg/s, but the specific impulse increases as the rocket climbs because the atmospheric pressure \(p\) drops. A linear fit to the flight data gives specific impulse, in seconds:

\[I_{sp} = 305.0 - 40.6 \frac{p}{p_{sl}}\]

where \(p_{sl}\) is the sea-level pressure (so the specific impulse is 264.4 s on the pad and 305.0 s in vacuum). The thrust can then be computed from the propellant flow rate and the specific impulse. All five engines burn until \(t = 135.2\) s, when the center engine is shut down. The other four continue until \(t = 161.6\) s, which is the end of the stage 1 burn.

The total rocket mass at liftoff is \(2.90 \times 10^6\) kg.

For the drag use a reference area of \(S_{ref} = 79.5\) m\(^2\) (the cross-sectional area of the first stage). The density and speed of sound change with altitude. For these, and for the pressure above, you can use the curve fit to the standard atmosphere from the textbook (it is intended for altitudes below 47 km, but above that the air is so thin that the differences are negligible). The drag coefficient changes significantly with Mach number. The values below are from a NASA preflight trajectory analysis of the Saturn V:

\(M\) 0.53 0.75 1.0 1.25 1.5 2.0 3.0 4.0 5.0 7.0
\(C_D\) 0.35 0.51 0.85 0.85 0.67 0.54 0.43 0.34 0.27 0.17

Interpolate linearly between these points, and use the end values outside of this range (this is what numpy.interp does). As a check, the drag should peak at about 2.1 MN near \(t = 72\) s.

You could solve this using any ODE solver (e.g. scipy.integrate.solve_ivp in python), or you could write a basic forward Euler method. This means that you set up a time vector, and a starting point for \(V, m, z, x\) (the rocket starts at rest on the pad), and then execute a for loop. At iteration (\(i\)) you update those four values using data from the previous iteration (\(i - 1\)). For example, using the last ODE (and setting \(\Delta t = t^{(i)} - t^{(i-1)}\)):

\[x^{(i)} = x^{(i-1)} + V^{(i-1)}\sin\theta^{(i-1)} \Delta t\]
